## Context

For a clinic I built three products on a shared base:

- **Contact center:** AI-assisted messaging over Messenger and Instagram, with a knowledge base and handoff to staff.
- **Suite:** the internal web app, with users, roles, sessions, auditing, metrics and a manageable knowledge base.
- **Patient memory:** for the medical directors, sourced from Google Drive, with interchangeable AI providers (OpenAI or Gemini) and a monitor with a timeline, labs, vital signs and alerts, designed and tested with synthetic data.

Each product has two variants: `local/`, on Docker Compose, and `aws/`, with a SAM template.

## The contact center’s guarantees

- **Never lose a message:** the webhook only acknowledges Meta once the message is queued.
- **Never reply twice:** idempotency plus a per-conversation lock with expiry.
- **Don’t reply halfway:** if the person keeps typing, the debounce waits and the responder checks there’s no newer message.
- **Clinical gate:** an explicit filter, in code, before the AI replies.

Node 20 with ESM and `node:http`, no frameworks. The suite uses HttpOnly, SameSite=Strict cookies with double-submit CSRF.

## Cost and availability, in writing

- **Serverless versus a single server.** I compared a single server (Lightsail), EC2, DigitalOcean, Hetzner, Azure and AWS service by service. AWS serverless won on availability: it costs about USD 25 more a month than the single server (estimate, list price), almost all of it Aurora’s minimum, but there’s no single server that can take everything down. The database and the queue sit behind adapters to avoid lock-in.
- **A cost ceiling on Aurora:** a 0.5 ACU minimum with no pause and a 2 ACU maximum with an alarm. The test environment pauses after 10 minutes.
- **30-day retention** for conversation text, with a scheduled purge. Less health data exposed, in exchange for losing historical detail.

## Verification

Test cases in every product, documented failure modes, a cost model, clinical-safety notes and scripts that check no demo data is left behind.

## What I learned

In healthcare, the hard part isn’t getting the AI to answer. It’s deciding **when it shouldn’t**, and keeping that decision in verifiable code rather than in a prompt.
